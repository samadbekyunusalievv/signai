Modern AI assistants — ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude — assume the user can read, type, listen, and speak. That assumption excludes tens of millions of people who communicate primarily in sign language. This document quantifies the gap and the opportunity.

2 · The Deaf Population, Broken Down

Hearing loss exists on a spectrum. The global figure most commonly cited — 1.5 billion — counts everyone with any measurable loss. The clinically relevant population for an AI accessibility product is much narrower:

Any hearing loss (1.5B)

Sign as primary language (~24M)

SignAI's core user is the 70M-person signing deaf community. Within that, roughly 24M use sign as their primary, native language — the people for whom an AI text box is genuinely a foreign-language interface.

3 · The Literacy Gap Is the Real Story

Even where deaf people can technically read, fluency is uneven. This is not a comment on intelligence — it's structural. Spoken languages encode sound; deaf children who never hear English struggle to map written English to anything internal.

"Only ~20% of deaf adults are fluent in written English. In some regions illiteracy among deaf adults exceeds 75%." — Sign.MT and supporting accessibility studies

Implication: a product that requires the user to type fluent English (which is what every chatbot today requires) excludes the majority of its potential signing users before they even open the app.

What "sign-only" actually means

The user originally asked about people who exclusively use sign — who cannot speak, text, read, or listen. The truthful answer from accessibility research is that pure exclusivity is rare; most deaf adults have some written-language skill even if limited. But functional sign-first users — people for whom signing is faster, more comfortable, and more accurate than reading or typing — are common, and they are the ones being shut out today. The honest hackathon framing: "tens of millions of signing-first users currently have a much worse AI experience than hearing users do."

The hearing 83% is not noise — those are interpreters, family members, hearing children of deaf adults (CODAs), educators, and ASL students. They are also the easiest "second audience" for SignAI: people who already want better tools to communicate with deaf signers.

5 · Why AI Doesn't Reach Them Today

  • Text-only input. Every major chatbot UI is a text box. Sign-first users either type slowly in a language they're not fluent in, or skip the product entirely.
  • Text-only output. Even when the model writes the answer, the user reads it in the same imperfect second language. Comprehension drops.
  • Voice features don't help. Voice-mode chatbots assume hearing. They are explicitly inaccessible to this audience.
  • Captions are not sign language. Captions are still written English. They help mid-loss users; they do not solve the problem for sign-first users.
  • No production-grade ASL→text or text→ASL avatar in major LLM products. The infrastructure simply doesn't exist in the consumer AI stack.

Where SignAI is different

Every existing product is a one-way pipe — usually text → avatar. None of them close the loop with a real AI conversation: camera → sign recognition → LLM → avatar response. That's the opening, and it's the entire premise of this hackathon submission.

Beyond direct users, the secondary market is large: schools for the deaf, government accessibility mandates (ADA, EAA in EU), enterprise compliance, and customer-service deployments are all distribution channels with real budgets.

8 · Why Now

  • Hand tracking is solved. MediaPipe Hands runs in-browser, on-device, at 30+ fps. Recent academic results report 95–99%+ accuracy for ASL alphabet/static-sign recognition with MediaPipe + lightweight classifiers.
  • LLMs are cheap and fast. A 2-second turnaround from sign → text → response → animation is achievable with a small distilled model.
  • 3D avatar tech is consumer-grade. Three.js, Unity WebGL, and rigged 3D characters work in any modern browser.
  • Regulation is tightening. The EU Accessibility Act (in force June 2025) and updated ADA Title II web-accessibility rules make sign-language access a near-term compliance issue, not a "nice to have."
  • Nothing in the major AI stack does this yet. First-mover window is open.

9 · Conclusion

The signing deaf population is large enough to matter (~70M), underserved enough to be obvious (no major chatbot supports sign), and reachable through proven channels (schools, accessibility orgs, enterprise compliance). The technical building blocks exist; nobody has assembled them into a two-way AI loop. SignAI is that assembly.
